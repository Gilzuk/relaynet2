\chapter{Summary}\label{sec:summary}

This work examined classical and neural network–based relay strategies for \textit{two-hop relay communication} using a unified simulation framework. The objective was to analyze performance–complexity tradeoffs and determine when learning-based relays provide practical advantages over analytically derived methods.
The core comparison uses one canonical setup (two-hop SISO, i.i.d.\ Rayleigh fast fading, QPSK, uncoded) and varies only the relay function; the coded and unknown-channel studies depart from it deliberately. The study showed the following:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The best tested learned relays outperform the implemented block-normalized AF at the evaluated low-SNR points (H1). Against conventional finite-power AF the MLP's 0~dB margin is only $0.003$--$0.007$, depending on the gain rule, and the sequence relays fall behind; the feedforward relays track DF, so the advantage is that of regeneration rather than of learning.
    \item Classical \textit{decode-and-forward (DF)} matched or outperformed all neural approaches at \textbf{medium-to-high SNR} while requiring no trainable parameters; no learned relay was detectably better, which supports H2 in this matching sense.
    \item Increasing model size did not produce a \textbf{systematic BER improvement}, and the replicated size sweep found no robust upward BER trend with model capacity (H3).
    \item The 169-parameter MLP is competitive with larger models in the original canonical comparison. This is not a universal minimum-size result, and no train/test-gap study establishes overfitting as the explanation.
    \item The replicated size sweep identifies the smallest tested configurations meeting each degradation criterion: four parameters suffice for three memoryless cases at zero degradation, while the selected three-tap cases use 73--145. Window length matters in this grid, but the experiment does not prove architecture lower bounds.
    \item At an equal parameter budget, feedforward and sequence relays remained separated at low SNR. The apparent high-SNR convergence was smaller than the Transformer run-to-run spread and is not seed-robust; equalizing parameters also left depth, width, state dimension, window, and optimization confounded (H4).
    \item Within the simulated benchmark, a \texttt{hybrid relay} combining neural processing at low SNR with DF at high SNR tracked the best evaluated relay; its uncalibrated received-power SNR estimate put the switch between 6 and 8~dB rather than at the nominal 5~dB. Threshold robustness, quantization, energy use, synchronization and hardware behavior were not evaluated.
\end{enumerate}
    Findings 1--7 concern the canonical comparison (the size sweep of finding~5 spans several channel families); findings 8--11 summarize Chapter~\ref{sec:unknown-channels}, and the coded study is summarized after them.
\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{7}
    \item On the selected BPSK three-tap filter, AF and zero-threshold DF approach the derived $0.25$ floor, while the 170-parameter MLP substantially reduces BER through the fixed-budget 16-dB point. Matched genie MLSE leads over the resolved range; the interpolated required-SNR penalties are $0.26$, $1.18$, $1.88$, and $2.53$~dB at BER $10^{-1}$ through $10^{-4}$. These are not a constant offset. In the separate blind study, the family-trained MLP leads the evaluated CMA implementation up to 12~dB without test-time adaptation; from 14~dB the two are not resolved.

    \item The pilot-aided LS-plus-MLSE sweep at 10~dB found the classical detector ahead at 20 pilots and the MLP at 5, with no resolved difference at 10; its SNR dependence was not measured. This plug-in detector does not marginalize channel uncertainty, and the experiment does not isolate estimation variance from nonlinear model mismatch. The family-trained relay avoids test-time pilots and per-block estimation. The result is specific to the tested estimator, channel family, block lengths, and SNR.

    \item The separate coded-QPSK memory sweep uses a window-11, eight-hidden-unit, four-output MLP with 220 parameters and 208 MACs per symbol, retrained for each channel length. The historical $2M^L$ count describes the implemented joint trellis. Exact I/Q factorization is available for the real-tap channel and reduces the classical count to $2^{L+1}$: at seven taps, 256 rather than 32{,}768 multiplications per symbol. These are analytical counts, not new performance measurements. MLSE retains lower measured BER. Fixed architecture size does not establish unchanged accuracy for longer memories.

    \item \textbf{Latency did not separate the learned relay from sequence detection at the available resolution} on the tested channel. No BER difference was resolved among the tested traceback depths; a two-symbol MLSE setting had fewer symbols of decision delay than the window-11 relay's five and lower measured BER. The block relay required a full frame before producing output and showed no resolved accuracy gain in this experiment. This coded-memory study and the pilot-budget study address different resources in different configurations. They do not measure a common boundary or a quantitative distance between boundaries.
\end{enumerate}

Read against the two research questions of Section~\ref{sec:two-objectives}, these findings give the following answers. \textbf{O1 (no budget):} on the evaluated channels with memory, the learned relay complements the classical method where the classical relay lacks the channel model --- against memoryless AF and zero-threshold DF, and against the plug-in LS-plus-MLSE detector once pilots are scarce --- and does not where matched MLSE is available, which retains the lower error rate (findings 8 and 9). \textbf{O2 (latency and arithmetic budget):} on the tested coded-memory channel, latency did not separate the learned relay from sequence detection (finding 11), and the arithmetic argument depends on how the trellis is implemented: the exactly factorized detector needs fewer multiplications than the 208-MAC network up to six taps and $1.23$ times as many at seven (finding 10). Within the tested configurations, neither budget therefore excludes the classical method.

The evidence supports targeted use of learned relays, not a general learned-versus-classical verdict. The measured advantages depend on model mismatch, available channel information, architecture, and budget. Reduced-state sequence estimation, decision-feedback equalization, and a coded destination metric that also models relay decision errors remain unmeasured alternatives.

The supplementary coded study first found the learned relays ahead of block DF at high SNR, but its decoder metrics ignored symbol-dependent post-equalization reliability. With matched metrics at the relay and the destination, block DF has lower BER than both learned relays at every tested SNR, in every paired trial; at 20~dB it makes 3 errors in $3\times10^{6}$ bits against $2{,}667$ for the best learned relay. The original high-SNR advantage was therefore produced by the metric mismatch. The matched relay decoder uses $|h_1|^2$, which the learned relays are not given; a learned relay with that input was not evaluated. The rate-adaptation experiment compares two classical schemes on a finite grid. With matched metrics, decoding at the relay gives the higher goodput at every SNR where a code is used, except under tight idealized buffering budgets (at 150 symbols below 20~dB, and at 100 symbols throughout), where block-DF's frame buffering excludes the codes it needs; neither is a neural-relay deployment guarantee (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
