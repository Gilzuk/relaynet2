\section{Canonical Relay Comparison (SISO, Rayleigh, QPSK)}\label{sec:siso-bpsk-performance-baseline-relay-comparison}

\textbf{Goal:} Evaluate the classical and AI-based relay strategies on the canonical setup of this thesis.

\textbf{Configuration.} SISO on both hops; QPSK; i.i.d.\ Rayleigh fast fading (canonical); one-tap zero-forcing equalization per hop, with no ISI equalizer (the channel is memoryless); six learned relays: supervised (MLP, Hybrid), generative (VAE) and sequence (Transformer and the simplified Mamba-S6 and Mamba-2 SSD of Section~\ref{sec:relay-strategies}), with tanh heads (the three sequence models then add the raw centre sample); training and run settings as executed are in Table~\ref{tbl:canonical-runs}.

\textbf{Conclusion:} In the historical canonical run, AI relays match but do not surpass symbol-wise DF: DF achieves the lowest or tied-lowest BER at essentially every SNR point, including the low-SNR regime, while the best neural relays (MLP, Hybrid, VAE) stay within $0.004$ of it in BER, are never resolvably better than it, and clearly outperform AF. This supports H1 (some AI relays beat AF at low SNR) and, in its matching sense, H2: at $\geq 6$~dB DF matches or beats every learned relay on the canonical channel, with zero trainable parameters. The AF column is the block-normalized implementation of Section~\ref{sec:evaluated-configurations}. A conventional finite-power, variable-gain AF with coherent destination processing reaches $0.3418$, $0.2450$ and $0.1437$ at 0, 4 and 8~dB, so against it the MLP's 0~dB margin is $0.007$ rather than $0.073$; a fixed-gain AF does better at 0~dB ($0.3384$), leaving $0.003$ (Section~\ref{sec:low-snr-advantage-of-neural-relays-h1-confirmed}).

Two features of Table~\ref{tbl:table2} deserve comment. First, symbol-wise DF tracks the closed form (the ``DF th.'' column above): reading the abscissa as $E_s/N_0$ and converting to $E_b/N_0$ for QPSK ($k=2$, so $3.01$~dB lower), the two-hop expression $2P(1-P)$ with $P=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\sqrt{\bar\gamma/(1+\bar\gamma)}\bigr)$ gives agreement to within a few percent relative error at every tabulated point ($1.0\%$ at 20~dB, $2.5\%$ at 16~dB, worst case $3.6\%$ at 12~dB, each recomputable from the four-decimal values as printed), which provides a consistency check on the channel, equalization and hard-decision pipeline against the analysis of Section~\ref{sec:rayleigh-two-hop-df-ber}. Figure~\ref{fig:fig10} plots this closed form alongside the measured curves, together with the single-hop floor $P$ itself -- the BER a genie that recovered hop~1 perfectly would still incur on hop~2 -- which is a reference for this uncoded fixed-rate QPSK comparison; no evaluated curve crosses it.

Second, in this historical run the relays divide by family rather than by paradigm. The three feedforward relays, the MLP, the Hybrid and the VAE, stay within $0.004$ of DF in BER at every SNR, but they are not indistinguishable from it. Paired over the ten shared trials (Section~\ref{sec:statistical-significance-testing}, uncorrected), DF has the lower BER at seven of the eleven SNRs against the MLP and against the VAE, and at one against the Hybrid, and no feedforward relay is resolvably better than DF anywhere~\coderef{paired}; among themselves, the Hybrid is ahead of the VAE at every point from 0 to 12~dB. Where no difference is resolved this is not a finding of equivalence: failure to reject requires a prespecified practical margin and an equivalence test to become one, and neither was performed. The historical VAE reaches $0.00972$ at 20~dB, matching DF at this budget; the rerun does not (Section~\ref{sec:round2-rerun-validation}). The three sequence models are the ones that separate, trailing by roughly $0.07$ in BER at 0~dB and by under $0.002$ from 16~dB, a gap still resolved at 20~dB for the Transformer and Mamba-S6. Raw parameter count does not explain it, since the sequence models are the larger of the two groups. One reading is that a memoryless channel offers no temporal structure for their inductive bias to exploit; another is that their training protocols, which differ from those of the feedforward relays and were not tuned per architecture, suit this task less well; a third is that all were trained on BPSK over AWGN and meet $\sqrt{2}$-smaller, heavy-tailed inputs here. The equal-parameter comparison of Section~\ref{sec:parameter-normalization-complexity-trade-off} removes the difference in parameter count but cannot separate these readings.

\paragraph{Why QPSK and not BPSK.} QPSK places one bit on each axis the Rayleigh coefficient acts in, carries two bits per symbol, and is processed by the I/Q-split relays without modification, which makes it the constellation that exercises the complex-signal processing this thesis studies. Coherent BPSK over the same complex channel would be entirely valid (after channel-phase removal the whole BPSK component lies on the real decision axis, so projecting onto it discards no signal energy) and the choice here is one of experimental design rather than of correctness (Section~\ref{sec:evaluated-configurations}). One consequence must be kept in view when reading these BER figures against BPSK closed forms: the SNR axis here is $E_s/N_0$, and QPSK carries two bits per symbol, so a given abscissa corresponds to an $E_b/N_0$ that is $3.01$~dB lower than the same number on a BPSK axis.

\section{Parameter Normalization \& Complexity Trade-off}\label{sec:parameter-normalization-complexity-trade-off}

This study tests H3 (complexity versus BER) and H4 (architecture convergence at an equal parameter budget) on the canonical setup.

\textbf{Goal:} Compare the architectures at an approximately equal nominal parameter count, and characterize the complexity-performance trade-off for relay denoising. Equal counts do not isolate inductive bias: width, depth, state dimension, window and the training protocols still differ (Section~\ref{sec:normalized-parameter-comparison}).

\textbf{Configuration.} As in Section~\ref{sec:siso-bpsk-performance-baseline-relay-comparison}, with every learned relay normalized to about 3{,}000 parameters, alongside the original sizes (169 to 26K parameters).

\textbf{Conclusion.} The 169-parameter MLP is competitive with the larger models in the original canonical comparison. At approximately 3,000 parameters, the cross-model relative BER spread, (max $-$ min)/max, falls from $17.4\%$ at 0~dB to $4.1\%$ at 20~dB. This does not isolate parameter count as the cause: width, depth, state dimensions, the context actually used (the Mamba relays read only the past half of their window) and optimizer settings also differ, although the run trained every model on the same $25{,}000$ samples for 100 epochs (Table~\ref{tbl:canonical-runs}), so the result describes these architectures under these training protocols. Later seed replication shows that the apparent high-SNR convergence is not robust to every initialization (Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}).

\section{Minimum Relay Size Across Channel Families}\label{sec:minimum-relay-size}

The complexity study of Section~\ref{sec:parameter-normalization-complexity-trade-off} finds no evaluated model detectably better than the 169-parameter MLP except the two Hybrids, at a few SNRs and by at most $0.0021$, but leaves two questions open: how far \emph{below} 169 parameters the relay can be taken, and whether the answer is a property of the architecture or of the channel. This section answers both by sweeping relay size downward on every channel family used in this thesis.

\textbf{Goal:} Locate the smallest relay that costs nothing measurable against a reference relay trained on the same channel, and determine which architectural axis --- window, depth or width --- sets that floor.

\textbf{Configuration.} SISO, two hops, Hop~2 following each channel family's own convention (AWGN or coherently compensated Rayleigh); BPSK or QPSK per channel; the nine channel families of Chapters~\ref{sec:experiments} and~\ref{sec:unknown-channels} plus the coded scenario of Section~\ref{sec:coded-block-df}; comparators symbol-wise DF on memoryless channels, Viterbi MLSE on channels with memory and block DF on the coded scenario, plus a reference relay (a window-5 MLP-169 retrained per channel, or MLP-756 coded); window $\in\{1,3,5,7\}$, depth $\in\{1,2,3\}$, width 1 to 48; three initializations per configuration, each held to its worst; metric: the additional SNR needed to reach a given BER.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Within the tested grid and degradation criteria, observation-window length is an important resource for the selected three-tap channels. Three of the five memoryless cases meet the zero-degradation criterion with four parameters, whereas the three-tap cases use 73--145. Widening the window from 1 to 7 improves the reported metric by more than 8~dB on every three-tap channel, a lower bound because no window-1 seed reaches every target the comparator reaches; added depth does not improve the best tested configuration. These are smallest \emph{tested} configurations under finite training and evaluation budgets, not mathematical lower bounds on architecture size. The budget-by-budget table (Table~\ref{tbl:table-minsize}), the seed spreads and the two figures that show the window effect (Figures~\ref{fig:minsize-crossover} and~\ref{fig:minsize-budget}) are in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-f-minimum-size}.

\section{Coded Block-DF: Measuring the Remark~\ref{rem:df-terminology} Caveat}\label{sec:coded-block-df}

\paragraph{A note on constellation scope.} The fixed-MCS relay-decoding comparisons here are QPSK throughout. 16-QAM appears only as a rung of the modulation-and-coding ladder in Section~\ref{sec:coded-rate-adaptation}, where the object of study is the rate-and-modulation choice rather than the relay's ability to process a denser constellation; that question is left to future work (Section~\ref{sec:modulation-schemes}).

Remark~\ref{rem:df-terminology} cautions that the symbol-wise DF results ``should not be read as bounds on coded block-DF performance''. That is asserted there and measured here, by a practical finite-length block-DF relay built from a rate-1/2 convolutional code and a soft-decision Viterbi decoder, as a supplementary study on the canonical channel rather than a revision of the core comparison.

\textbf{Setup.} A standard rate-1/2 convolutional code (constraint length $K$, $2^{K-1}$ trellis states, zero-tail terminated per 200-information-bit frame) is applied before QPSK modulation on the canonical Rayleigh channel; the coded block-DF relay~\coderef{relaynet} decodes the full frame at the relay via soft-decision Viterbi, then re-encodes and re-modulates a fresh codeword before forwarding. Because post-equalization noise is heteroskedastic, the decoders weight each symbol by its instantaneous channel reliability (the matched metric); these are the results of the section (Section~\ref{sec:coded-matched-metrics}). The published pipeline used an unweighted squared-distance metric instead and is kept as a diagnostic (Section~\ref{sec:coded-unweighted}). Two coded-aware learned relays are trained on the same task for comparison: a windowed 756-parameter MLP classifier (window 21) and the Mamba-S6 architecture already used in Table~\ref{tbl:table2} (24,084 parameters at this configuration), both predicting the transmitted QPSK symbol from a window of noisy coded observations with no explicit knowledge of the code. Unless a table states otherwise, the unweighted results use 100 trials $\times$ 100,000 information bits per SNR point, and the matched reruns the first 30 of those trials.

\subsection{Matched Decoder Metrics: The Principal Coded Comparison}\label{sec:coded-matched-metrics}

\paragraph{Learned-relay training.} The MLP uses $42\to16\to4$ layers, tanh hidden units and softmax outputs. Training uses $2{,}000$ frames at each of 5, 10 and 15~dB: $1{,}212{,}000$ labeled windows, padded only at each SNR stream's ends. Inputs are equalized QPSK observations, with no axis rescaling; the code is $K=3$, generators $(7,5)$ in octal, two zero tail bits. Cross-entropy is optimized in NumPy float64 for 25 epochs, batch 512, Adam learning rate $0.003$, $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.999)$, $\epsilon=10^{-8}$, no scheduler or clipping. Final weights are used, with no validation split. Initialization and training-data seeds are 0; shuffle seed is 42. Evaluation uses $800000+1000\,\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}+t$, $t=0,\ldots,29$~\coderef{matched}.

The zero-forced observation $y/h = x + n/h$ has noise variance $1/(2\gamma|h|^{2})$ per real axis, which changes from symbol to symbol. The matched metric weights each squared residual by $|h|^{2}$, which is the same as decoding $|y - hx|^{2}$ on the unequalized observation, and it is the comparison this section reports as its result. The hard/soft MLP and classical comparisons are rerun with it~\coderef{matched}; Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-coded-diagnostics} retains the original unweighted results, including the unmatched Mamba comparison. The relay Viterbi decoder weights its branch metrics by $|h_1|^{2}$; the soft relay's BCJR uses the per-symbol variance $1/(2\gamma|h_1|^{2})$; and the destination weights by $|h_2|^{2}$, or for AF by the end-to-end variance, which requires both hops' gains. The learned relays evaluated are the hard and soft read-outs of the 756-parameter MLP, unchanged and still given no channel gain; only their destination metric changes, and both destination metrics are reported. The Mamba-coded relay of the unweighted study was not rerun with matched metrics, so every statement below concerns the two MLP read-outs. The run uses the first 30 of the 100 paired trials of Table~\ref{tbl:table39}, each of $100{,}000$ information bits. Its unweighted rows reproduce that table's per-trial values exactly. The 30-trial budget was retained after inspecting running means, so the paired tests below are exploratory; the within-trial differences isolate the metric change.

\textbf{With matched metrics, block DF leads at every tested SNR.} Matched hard block DF has lower BER than both MLP read-outs at all six SNRs from 0 to 20~dB, in all 30 paired trials at each point (Wilcoxon $p<10^{-5}$), whichever destination metric the MLP is given. At 8~dB it reaches $0.0032$ against the better MLP's $0.0619$. At 20~dB it makes 3 errors in $3\times10^{6}$ bits ($1.0\times10^{-6}$), against $2{,}667$ for the soft MLP ($8.9\times10^{-4}$), and stays within a factor of $2.4$ of the clean-codeword oracle at every SNR. The fresh validation is reported in Section~\ref{sec:round2-rerun-validation}; the numerical values and paired tests here describe the historical run. Soft block DF with the matched BCJR is no better than hard block DF: it is worse at 0--8~dB and not resolved from it at 12--20~dB.

\textbf{The weighted destination metric does not help the MLP read-outs at high SNR.} Weighting by $|h_2|^{2}$ is matched to the second hop only. It lowers the MLP read-outs' BER below 12~dB but raises it from 12~dB for the hard MLP and from 16~dB for both, consistent with giving high confidence to a wrong relay symbol on a strong second hop; that mechanism was not isolated by an ablation. A destination metric that also models relay errors, and a learned relay given $|h_1|$, were not evaluated. Either way the MLP read-outs remain far behind matched block DF.

\textbf{Consequences for this section.} The published coded tables used an unweighted metric, under which the learned relays appeared ahead of block DF at high SNR. Changing only the metric reverses that ordering at every SNR for the two MLP read-outs, so the published comparisons established an advantage over a mismatched decoding pipeline, not a disadvantage of decoding and re-encoding at the relay. The short diagnostic discussion below points to the full unweighted tables in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-coded-diagnostics}. The other coded studies of this chapter, the error-location diagnostic (Table~\ref{tbl:table38}), the constraint-length sweep, the reliable-decoding regime (Table~\ref{tbl:table44}), the equal-throughput, rate-adaptation and readiness-budget studies (Tables~\ref{tbl:table40}, \ref{tbl:table42} and~\ref{tbl:table43}) and the coded size sweep, were rerun with the matched metric on their published seeds (30 trials~\coderef{matched-reruns}), and their matched results are given first where each appears. The coded-memory study of Section~\ref{sec:joint-latency-memory} has no fading, so per-symbol reliability weights do not arise there.

\paragraph{Constraint-length and higher-SNR checks.} With the matched metric (\coderef{matched-reruns}, 30 trials) larger $K$ helps from 4~dB upward: at 8~dB the BER is $0.0033$, $0.00069$ and $6.7\times10^{-5}$ for $K=3,5,7$. At 16 and 20~dB, $K=5,7$ make no errors in $3\times10^6$ bits; these are finite-budget observations, not zero expected BER. At 0~dB larger $K$ remains worse. In the higher-SNR $K=3$ rerun (30 trials of $2\times10^5$ bits), matched block DF and the matched oracle each make one bit error at 20~dB and none at 24--32~dB. Both MLP training variants remain at $1.1\times10^{-3}$ to $3.7\times10^{-5}$ under their better destination metric. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-coded-diagnostics} retains the unweighted diagnostic results.

\subsection{Diagnostic: The Published Unweighted Pipeline}\label{sec:coded-unweighted}

The original unweighted comparison included the MLP and simplified Mamba-S6 (Tables~\ref{tbl:table34} and~\ref{tbl:table37}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-coded-diagnostics}). At 20~dB both have lower BER than unweighted block DF in at least 99 of 100 paired trials; at 16~dB Mamba wins 68, while the MLP wins 23 and loses 77. This describes that mismatched pipeline. Matched decoding reverses the ordering for the two evaluated MLP read-outs; no matched Mamba result is available. The error-location diagnostic (Table~\ref{tbl:table38}) likewise does not establish a general penalty for decoding and re-encoding: matched block DF makes almost no relay-output errors at these SNRs.

\subsection{Soft-Decision Relaying under the Unweighted Metrics (Diagnostic)}\label{sec:coded-soft-decision}

The MLP softmax-weighted constellation average has lower measured BER than its hard argmax on the same weights at every sampled SNR under unweighted metrics (Table~\ref{tbl:table39}, Figure~\ref{fig:fig57}); its calibration was not tested. Unweighted soft block DF uses a nominal variance despite the varying $1/(2\gamma|h|^2)$ noise variance after equalization. Doubling that assumed variance removes its resolved 20-dB gap to hard block DF, but does not supply the missing per-symbol reliability. These diagnostic benefits do not establish a learned-relay advantage under matched decoding; both evaluated MLP read-outs remain behind matched block DF.

\subsection{Throughput and Latency: What the BER Tables Leave Out}\label{sec:coded-latency-throughput}

Equal $E_s/N_0$ does not by itself hold throughput or relay readiness fixed. The following controls account for these costs.

\textbf{Throughput: the coded comparison was not like-for-like.} A rate-1/2 code carries half the information per channel use. Concretely, the coded QPSK link spends $202$ symbols to deliver $200$ information bits ($0.99$ bits/symbol) where uncoded QPSK spends $100$ ($2.00$ bits/symbol). The $5.7\times$ BER gain at 16~dB in the unweighted Table~\ref{tbl:table34} is therefore bought with half the data rate, and a link designer given that trade would not read it as a $5.7\times$ improvement at all. The honest comparison holds spectral efficiency fixed, and the measurements already collected supply it: rate-1/2 \emph{16-QAM} delivers $4 \times \tfrac12 \times \tfrac{200}{202} \approx 1.98$ information bits per symbol, matching uncoded QPSK's $2.00$. Table~\ref{tbl:table40} places those two side by side.

\textbf{Relay readiness: block relays buffer substantially more.} The relays differ in how much they must accumulate before producing their first output. Symbol-wise AF and DF can process a current symbol --- a property of the ideal symbol-wise rules with a fixed or statistically known gain; the implemented AF estimates its gain from the empirical power of the whole simulated block (Section~\ref{sec:evaluated-configurations}), which is block-adaptive and not symbol-causal, so the zero in Table~\ref{tbl:table41} describes the algorithmic rule, not that implementation detail; the symmetric 21-sample learned relay needs $w=10$ future symbols; and block-DF must receive a 202-symbol frame before decoding. An implementable streaming rule (a fixed gain, or a running power estimate) was not evaluated. Second, the 202-symbol figure belongs to the implemented full-frame decoder; a convolutional decoder with finite traceback can release decisions after a fixed delay without waiting for the frame, so it is not a requirement of every coded relay. These are relay-processing readiness delays, not complete end-to-end latency: in the half-duplex protocol every strategy must still spend channel uses on both source--relay and relay--destination transmissions.

\subsection{Adaptive Modulation and Coding: Choosing the Rate, Not Just the Relay}\label{sec:coded-rate-adaptation}

Rate adaptation chooses the modulation-and-coding scheme (MCS) with the highest useful rate at each SNR from a finite grid. The mother code of Section~\ref{sec:coded-block-df} is extended here with the standard punctured rates $2/3$ and $3/4$ (deleting a periodic subset of the rate-1/2 output, re-inserted as soft zeros at the decoder, so the same Viterbi decoder runs unmodified at every rate), and evaluated via bit-interleaved coded modulation across the full grid $\{\text{QPSK}, \text{16-QAM}\} \times \{\text{uncoded}, 1/2, 2/3, 3/4\}$.

The idealized one-shot \emph{goodput}, $G=\eta(1-\mathrm{FER})$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:goodput}), assumes perfect frame-error detection and discards failed frames without feedback or retransmission overhead. Both $G$ and $\eta$ are per active-hop channel symbol, as is the capacity reference $C$ below. Accounting for both half-duplex slots halves these rates without changing the preferred MCS or relay. Maximizing $G$ over the grid at each SNR gives the adaptation envelope for \emph{block-DF}, which decodes and re-encodes at the relay, and \emph{denoise-only}, which hard-decides bits and re-modulates without code knowledge or training. The latter retains the transmitted code constraints for destination decoding.

\textbf{The envelope is a real staircase, and its top step is ``turn the code off''.} With the matched metric, block DF climbs QPSK~1/2~$\to$~QPSK~2/3~$\to$~16-QAM~1/2~$\to$~16-QAM~3/4~$\to$~\emph{16-QAM uncoded} as SNR rises from 8 to 40~dB; with the unweighted metric both relay strategies climb QPSK~1/2~$\to$~QPSK~3/4~$\to$~16-QAM~2/3~$\to$~16-QAM~3/4~$\to$~\emph{16-QAM uncoded}; each switch occurs once the next rate's threshold has cleared. The remaining gap below $4$ depends on frame errors; the contribution of other relay designs was not measured.

\textbf{With the matched metric, decoding at the relay matters.} Block-DF's matched envelope (matched column of Table~\ref{tbl:table42}) is far above both published envelopes at every SNR where a code is used: $0.66$ against $0.0003$ information bits/symbol at 8~dB, $0.99$ against $0.13$ at 12~dB, $1.52$ against $0.60$ at 16~dB, $1.90$ against $0.96$ at 20~dB and $2.35$ against $1.47$ at 24~dB, and it reaches each higher-rate MCS at a lower SNR. The $|h_2|^{2}$ weight models second-hop reliability but not the denoise relay's decision errors. Above 32~dB both strategies select 16-QAM uncoded. Neither comparison is neural versus classical.

\textbf{With the unweighted metric (diagnostic), relay strategy barely mattered.} The two published envelopes in Table~\ref{tbl:table42} differ by at most $0.105$ information bits/symbol (at 24~dB, under $7\%$ of the envelope value) and are identical from 36~dB, where both select 16-QAM uncoded, whereas the MCS choice moves goodput by $1.25$ at 24~dB and by more than $1.7$ from 32~dB. That finding is a property of the unweighted decoder.

\subsection{Goodput Under an Idealized Buffer-Readiness Budget}\label{sec:coded-latency-capacity}

The coded relay of Section~\ref{sec:coded-block-df} decodes a whole frame before forwarding, and every comparison so far has left the frame length free. A link with a deadline cannot: the deadline bounds the blocklength and with it the redundancy the relay has to spend. The achievable rate once blocklength is fixed is a standard question --- the finite-blocklength capacity of Polyanskiy, Poor and Verd\'{u} \cite{PolyanskiyPoorVerdu2010FiniteBlocklength} and the delay-limited capacity of Hanly and Tse \cite{HanlyTse1998DelayLimited} for fading channels.

The following calculation uses an idealized \emph{buffer-readiness budget}, not physical round-trip latency. It counts one destination frame buffer for both strategies and an additional relay frame buffer for block-DF, while assigning a ten-symbol look-ahead allowance to denoise-only relaying. It omits the channel-use duration of the two half-duplex transmission phases, which both strategies incur. Re-deriving the goodput envelope of Table~\ref{tbl:table42} under this accounting illustrates the effect of relay buffering, but does not establish performance under a complete end-to-end deadline.

\textbf{Tighten the budget further and the reversal spreads to SNRs where block-DF led unconstrained.} At $L_{\max}=100$ symbols, block-DF has \emph{no} coded option left at any of these four SNRs -- every one of its coded MCS choices exceeds the budget -- so it is pinned to uncoded transmission throughout, while the denoise-only relay still reaches 16-QAM~2/3 or~3/4 at 20, 24 and 28~dB. This structural lock-out does not depend on the decoder metric: with matched metrics block-DF is still pinned to uncoded transmission at 100 symbols, and denoise-only leads at 24 and 28~dB by $1.46\times$ and $1.52\times$.

The read-out comparison of Section~\ref{sec:coded-soft-decision} does not establish a decoding-location mechanism. The finite-grid results show sensitivity to rate selection and idealized buffering. A hard-deadline deployment recommendation requires a slot-level model of both half-duplex transmissions, processing time and reliability; that analysis is not performed here.
